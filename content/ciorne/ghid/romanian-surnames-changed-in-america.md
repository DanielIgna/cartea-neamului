Many families in the United States and Canada carry a surname that sounds Romanian, but not quite. Your great-grandfather may have been born Ioan Moldovan in a village near Bistrița and died as John Moldavan in Ohio. Somewhere in between, a clerk, a teacher or the family itself changed the spelling.

If you want to find your Romanian ancestors, the most important thing to understand is this: **you are not looking for one name, you are looking for a family of spellings.** This guide explains how Romanian names changed on the way to North America, and how to search for every version.

## The Ellis Island myth

A popular story says that immigration officers at Ellis Island changed people's names on arrival. In fact, arrival lists were copied from the passenger lists made at the port of departure in Europe, where clerks wrote what was in the passport. The officers in New York checked names against that list; they did not invent new ones.

The real changes came later: at school, at work, in the census, when someone signed up for the army, and above all at naturalization, when many immigrants chose an easier-sounding name for themselves and their children.

## Why your ancestor's name appears in Hungarian or German

Before 1918, Transylvania, Banat, Crișana, Maramureș and Bucovina belonged to the Austro-Hungarian Empire. A Romanian from a village near Cluj traveled with a Hungarian passport, often with the name written the Hungarian way:

- *Ioan* could appear as **János**, *Gheorghe* as **György**, *Vasile* as **Vazul** or **László**;
- *Șandor* or *Sandor* was a common rendering of Alexandru;
- the village itself appeared under its Hungarian or German name, for example *Kolozsvár* for Cluj or *Nagyszeben* and *Hermannstadt* for Sibiu.

On the arrival list, the country of origin is often "Austria-Hungary" or "Hungary", while the column for "race or people" says "Roumanian". That column is often the first proof that the family was Romanian. Our Romanian guide on [documents in Hungarian and German](/ghid/documente-in-maghiara-si-germana) shows what to look for.

## How Romanian first names became English

First names changed most, usually to the closest English name:

- Ioan or Ion → **John**
- Gheorghe → **George**
- Nicolae → **Nicholas** or **Nick**
- Vasile → **William** or **Charles** (no direct equivalent, so families chose freely)
- Maria → **Mary**; Ana → **Ann** or **Anna**
- Ilie → **Elias** or **Elijah**; Petru → **Peter**; Mihai → **Michael**

Women often appear under their husband's surname only, so finding a grandmother's maiden name is a separate step. Our Romanian guide on [finding your grandmother's maiden name](/ghid/cum-gasesti-numele-de-fata-al-bunicii) helps.

## How surnames changed

Surnames changed in a few typical ways:

- **Spelling by ear:** Moldovan → Moldavan, Pop → Popp or Pope, Rus → Russ.
- **Dropped endings:** Popescu → Popesc or simply Pope; -escu and -eanu were often shortened.
- **Lost diacritics:** Ș, Ț, Ă, Î and Â became S, T and A, so *Țăranu* became *Taranu* or *Taran*.
- **Translation:** sometimes a name was translated into English, like Lupu → Wolf, Croitoru → Taylor.
- **Village nicknames:** in Romania, many families were known by a house nickname (*poreclă*). Some immigrants kept the nickname as their surname in America.

## How to search under every spelling

1. **Write down every version you know**, from family papers, gravestones, letters and naturalization records.
2. **Search by sound, not spelling.** Many databases offer Soundex or "similar names" options. Use them.
3. **Search with the village, not just the name.** The last residence on an arrival list narrows hundreds of results to a few.
4. **Look for the whole group.** People from the same village often traveled together and settled together, for example in Cleveland, Detroit, Chicago or in Saskatchewan. A neighbor's record can lead you to yours.
5. **Check the return trips.** Many Romanians crossed the ocean two or three times, so the same person can appear on several lists.

## An example, step by step

Say your family remembers a great-grandfather called "John Moldavan" who worked in a Youngstown steel mill and came "from near Bistrița" around 1910.

1. In the 1920 or 1930 US census you find John Moldavan, born about 1888, with Romanian as his mother tongue and 1909 as his year of arrival.
2. You search the 1909 arrival lists for every spelling: Moldovan, Moldavan, Moldován, and the first names John, Ioan and János. A "János Moldován" appears, from Austria-Hungary, "race or people" Roumanian, last residence a village near Beszterce, the Hungarian name of Bistrița.
3. You look up the village name today and find the Romanian commune. Its parish registers, kept by the county archives in Bistrița, show the baptism of Ioan Moldovan in 1888 and the names of his parents.

Three different spellings, one person. Without searching for all of them, the trail would have stopped in Ohio.

## What to write in your family tree

When you add an ancestor to your tree, write the name as it appears in Romanian records, then the North American form in brackets: "Ioan (John) Moldovan". Do the same for places: the village name today and the old Hungarian or German name.

On Cartea Neamului, relatives in Romania will recognize the Romanian form, while your children in the US or Canada will recognize the English one. Both point to the same person.

## Frequently asked questions

### Did Ellis Island officers change my ancestor's name?

Almost never. They checked names against lists made in Europe. Most changes happened later, in daily life or at naturalization.

### Why does my Romanian ancestor appear as Hungarian on the passenger list?

Because before 1918 Transylvania and Banat were part of Austria-Hungary. Look at the column for "race or people", which often says "Roumanian".

### How do I find the original Romanian spelling of my surname?

Start from the oldest document you have and from the family village. Parish registers in Romania show how the name was written there.

### My family name has no diacritics anymore. Should I add them back?

In your family tree, write both forms. Use the Romanian spelling for ancestors born in Romania and the form each person used in North America.

### Can I search Romanian records without speaking Romanian?

Yes, with a few key words and the village name. Our [guide for the US and Canada](/diaspora/statele-unite) lists where to start.
