Every immigrant family carries an invisible suitcase. Inside it are the memories of a homeland left behind, the sacrifices made to build a life in a new country, and the quiet resilience that shaped the next generation.

If you grew up in the United States, Canada or Western Europe as the child of Romanian or Eastern European immigrants, you probably know how easily those stories slip through the cracks of everyday life. Between work, school and busy family schedules, conversations with our parents stay focused on the present, until one day we realize we don't know how they met, what their childhood village looked like, or what it felt like to step off a plane into an unfamiliar world.

What we don't record today, our children will never know. Here is how to capture your immigrant parents' and grandparents' stories gently, meaningfully and for good.

## 1. Stop waiting for a "big interview"

One of the biggest reasons families put off recording their history is that it feels like a huge project. Sitting a parent down in front of a bright light with fifty formal questions often makes them freeze up or say, "I don't have anything interesting to tell."

Instead, keep it casual and conversational:

- **Use everyday moments.** Sunday dinners, long car rides or quiet phone calls are the best times to ask a single question.
- **Start with objects, not dates.** Pull out an old black-and-white photograph, a traditional embroidered blouse (*ie*) or a handwritten recipe card. Asking "Who is standing next to Grandma in this photo?" unlocks memories far faster than "Tell me about your life in the 1960s."
- **Let them speak in their native language.** Many immigrant parents express emotion, humor and nuance far more naturally in Romanian, or their mother tongue, than in English. Encourage them to speak in whichever language feels closest to their heart. You can translate later, together.
- **Ask one thing at a time.** A single good question, asked again a few weeks later, often brings back details that were missing the first time.

## 2. Capture their actual voice, not just written notes

Writing down a summary of your parents' stories is valuable, but hearing their voice is irreplaceable. Years from now, the rhythm of their laugh, their accent and the way they pause when remembering an old friend will mean more to your children than any typed paragraph.

You don't need studio equipment. Your smartphone is more than enough:

1. Place the phone flat on the table between you, so it doesn't feel intrusive.
2. Keep recordings short: even a two-to-five-minute story is a treasure.
3. Save each clip next to the person it belongs to, so it never gets buried in a lost phone backup or a forgotten folder.
4. Write one line about each recording: who is speaking, when and where. In fifty years, nobody will remember it otherwise.

## 3. Ten questions to ask your immigrant parents and grandparents

If you aren't sure where to begin, pick one or two of these for your next conversation:

1. What sounds and smells do you remember most from the village or town where you grew up?
2. Who was the most important person in your childhood: a grandparent, a teacher or a neighbor?
3. What was school like when you were my age, and what did you dream of becoming?
4. How did you and Mom (or Dad) first meet, and what was your wedding day like?
5. What was the hardest decision you had to make when leaving your home country?
6. What do you remember about your very first week in America or Canada?
7. Which traditions from back home were you most determined to pass on to us?
8. Is there a family recipe that has been passed down through generations?
9. When did you feel proudest of our family?
10. What is one piece of advice you want your grandchildren and great-grandchildren to remember?

## 4. Connect the dots for the next generation

For children born in North America, their grandparents' homeland can feel abstract: just a name on a map. When you pair your parents' stories with a visual family tree and a map of the places the family came from, that history becomes real.

Seeing that their great-grandparents lived in a specific village in Transylvania, Moldavia or Banat, reading the stories of cousins who stayed behind and seeing how every branch connects helps kids understand who they are and where they come from.

Because Cartea Neamului works in ten languages, including English and Romanian, grandparents in Romania and grandchildren in the US or Canada can open the exact same family tree, each with the site in the language they are most comfortable with. The stories and recordings stay in the language they were told in, which is often exactly what makes them precious.

## Frequently asked questions

### My parents say they have nothing interesting to tell. What do I do?

Start with an object or a photograph instead of a question about "your life". Specific things, like a house, a recipe or a person in a picture, bring back specific memories.

### Should I record in English or in Romanian?

In the language your parents speak most naturally. Emotion and humor come through best in the mother tongue, and you can add a short English summary under each recording for the grandchildren.

### How long should a recording be?

Short is better. Two to five minutes about one story is easier to record, easier to listen to and easier to find later than a two-hour interview.

### Who can listen to the recordings?

Only the relatives you invite to your family tree. Nothing is public unless you decide to share it.

### Can grandparents who are not comfortable with technology take part?

Yes. A grandchild or a neighbor can hold the phone and record, and the grandparent only has to talk.
