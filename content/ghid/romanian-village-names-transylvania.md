A family document says your great-grandfather was born in **Nagyszeben**, or **Kronstadt**, or a village in "Szolnok-Doboka". You look for it on a map of Romania and find nothing. That is normal: until 1918, Transylvania, Banat, Crișana and Maramureș were part of the Kingdom of Hungary, and almost every place had a Hungarian name, often a German one, and a Romanian one.

Emigrants who left before the First World War, and the clerks who wrote their papers in America or elsewhere, usually used the official Hungarian name. This guide shows how to turn that name into a place you can find today.

## The same town, three names

Some of the bigger towns, with today's Romanian name first:

- **Cluj-Napoca**: Hungarian *Kolozsvár*, German *Klausenburg*
- **Sibiu**: Hungarian *Nagyszeben*, German *Hermannstadt*
- **Brașov**: Hungarian *Brassó*, German *Kronstadt*
- **Târgu Mureș**: Hungarian *Marosvásárhely*, German *Neumarkt am Mieresch*
- **Oradea**: Hungarian *Nagyvárad*, German *Großwardein*
- **Timișoara**: Hungarian *Temesvár*, German *Temeswar*
- **Bistrița**: Hungarian *Beszterce*, German *Bistritz*
- **Sighișoara**: Hungarian *Segesvár*, German *Schäßburg*
- **Alba Iulia**: Hungarian *Gyulafehérvár*, German *Karlsburg*
- **Satu Mare**: Hungarian *Szatmárnémeti*, German *Sathmar*

Villages work the same way, but there are thousands of them, and many Hungarian names have nothing in common with the Romanian ones.

## Counties changed too

Before 1918, Transylvania and the lands around it were divided into Hungarian **counties** (*vármegye*, in Romanian *comitat*), such as Kolozs, Szeben, Fogaras, Szolnok-Doboka, Beszterce-Naszód, Temes or Krassó-Szörény. After 1918 Romania created its own counties, changed them again in 1950 into regions and districts, and in 1968 created the counties that exist today.

So a village recorded as being "in Szolnok-Doboka county" in 1905 may today be in Cluj, Sălaj or Bistrița-Năsăud county. Its registers are at the National Archives branch of the **present-day** county.

## Names that changed after 1918

Romania renamed many villages in the 20th century: some to replace Hungarian or German names, some to drop names considered unpleasant, some to honour people. In Moldavia and Wallachia, villages were also merged or renamed. The name your grandmother used in 1950 may already be gone.

## How to find the village today

1. **Write the name exactly as it appears**, with every accent: á, é, ő and ű matter in Hungarian.
2. **Look it up in a historical gazetteer.** The best-known for Transylvania is Coriolan Suciu's *Dicționar istoric al localităților din Transilvania* (1967–1968), which gives every Romanian, Hungarian and German name and the old administrative units. Hungarian gazetteers from before 1918 list the official names county by county.
3. **Check old maps.** The Austrian military surveys of the 18th and 19th centuries show villages under their names of the time. See our guide to [old Habsburg maps](/ghid/harti-vechi-ridicarile-austriece).
4. **Search our village pages.** Cartea Neamului has a page for every Romanian village, with its commune, county and archive; once you have the Romanian name, it tells you where to write.
5. **Ask the family in Romania.** Older people often still use the old names of nearby villages, especially in mixed areas.

## Records in Hungarian and German

Records from Transylvania and Banat between 1895 and 1918 are in Hungarian, and first names were translated: Ioan became János, Gheorghe became György, Iosif became József, Maria became Mária. Saxon and Swabian parish registers are in German. See our guide to [documents in Hungarian and German](/ghid/documente-in-maghiara-si-germana).

## Frequently asked questions

### My grandfather's papers say "Austria-Hungary". Was he Hungarian?

Not necessarily. Before 1918 Transylvania, Banat, Crișana and Maramureș were in Hungary, and Bukovina in Austria. Millions of Romanians were born there and appear in foreign records as "Hungarian" or "Austrian" by citizenship.

### Why can't I find the village on Google Maps?

Because you are probably searching for its old Hungarian or German name, or a name changed after 1918. Find the Romanian name in a historical gazetteer first.

### Which archive holds the records of a village in an old Hungarian county?

The county branch of the Romanian National Archives for the county the village belongs to today, not the old one.

### Are there online maps with the old names?

Yes. Historical maps of the Habsburg military surveys can be viewed online and show villages under their names of the time.

### Can I note all the names of the village in my tree?

Yes. On Cartea Neamului you can write the village's current name and keep the old forms in the person's notes, so every relative recognises it.
