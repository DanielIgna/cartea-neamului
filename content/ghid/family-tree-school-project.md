Sooner or later, almost every child brings home the same assignment: make a family tree. Sometimes it is three boxes for grandparents on a worksheet, sometimes a "heritage project" with a poster and a short talk in front of the class. For a child whose family came from Romania, it is also a chance to discover where they come from, and to hear stories that would otherwise stay with the grandparents.

This guide walks you through the project step by step: which template to use, how many generations to include, what to ask relatives, and how to handle the questions that make many parents hesitate.

## Step 1: Read the assignment and choose the size

Teachers usually ask for one of three things:

- **A three-generation tree:** the child, the parents and the four grandparents. Common in the early grades.
- **A four-generation tree:** adding the eight great-grandparents. Common when the class studies history, immigration or "where our families come from".
- **A heritage project:** a tree plus a story, a map, a recipe or an object from the family's country of origin, presented to the class.

Our [printable family tree](/unelte/arbore-printabil) has versions for three, four and five generations. Print the one that matches the assignment. If you are not sure, start with three generations: a small tree that is filled in completely looks better than a big one full of question marks.

## Step 2: Let the child fill in what they know

Put the child's name at the bottom and let them write the names they already know: parents, brothers and sisters, grandparents. Help only with spelling. Children remember more than adults expect, and the gaps become the questions for the next step.

For every person, the boxes usually hold:

- first name and surname;
- year of birth (and of death, if the person has died);
- the town or village where they were born.

### Romanian names on the tree

If the grandparents were born in Romania, write their names the Romanian way, with the diacritics if you can: Ioan, Gheorghe, Ștefan, Maria, Elena. Next to it, add the name they used in America, if it changed. Children are often proud to see their grandmother's real name written correctly.

For women, write the **maiden name**, the surname they were born with. It is the name that connects the tree to the mother's side of the family.

## Step 3: Ask the grandparents

This is the part children remember long after the grade. Help your child call or visit the grandparents with four or five questions:

1. Where were you born, and what was the house like?
2. What were your parents' names?
3. What games did you play as a child?
4. What was your first job?
5. Why did you come to America (or to Canada, or to England)?

Our list of [40 questions to ask your Romanian grandparents](/ghid/questions-to-ask-romanian-grandparents) has many more, each also written in Romanian, so the child can ask in the grandparents' language. If you can, record the answers on a phone. A two-minute recording of a grandmother telling a story is worth more than a page of notes, and it stays in the family.

## Step 4: Make it visual

For the poster or the presentation:

- add a small photo of each person, printed or drawn;
- put a map next to the tree and mark the village in Romania and the town where the family lives now;
- add one object or story from the old country: a recipe, a song, a word that only grandma uses.

Old photos are fragile. Scan or photograph them instead of gluing originals onto a poster; the guide on [preserving old photos](/ghid/cum-pastrezi-pozele-vechi) shows how.

## When the family is not a "standard" tree

Many parents worry about this part. Children live in all kinds of families: with one parent, with step-parents, with grandparents, adopted, with a parent who has died. Some practical rules:

- **The tree can show the family the child lives in.** Most teachers accept a tree with step-parents, adoptive parents or the grandparents who raise the child. If you are unsure, ask the teacher before the child starts.
- **Empty boxes are fine.** Write what you know, or "unknown", and move on. An empty box is not a failure; it is a question for later.
- **Let the child decide what to share in class.** A tree at home can hold more than the poster at school.

## After the project: keep the tree going

Most school trees end up in a drawer. A few become the start of something the whole family keeps building: cousins add their branches, grandparents add stories, and years later the child finds the tree again and knows where they come from.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many generations should a school family tree have?

Three generations (child, parents, grandparents) is enough for most assignments in the early grades. Older students studying history or immigration often add great-grandparents. Our guide on [how many generations you can find](/ghid/cate-generatii-poti-afla) explains what is realistic.

### What if we do not know the great-grandparents?

Leave the boxes empty or write what you know: a first name, a village, a year. Ask the oldest relatives, who often remember their own grandparents' names.

### Should my child write the Romanian names or the English ones?

Both, if they are different. The Romanian name is the one in the family's documents in Romania, and the English one is the one people used here.

### Is the printable template free?

Yes. The [printable family tree](/unelte/arbore-printabil) is free and needs no account.

### How do we handle a step-family or adoption?

Show the family the child lives in, and talk to the teacher if you are unsure. Most teachers want the child to feel comfortable, not to follow a fixed shape.
