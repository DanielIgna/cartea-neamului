Many people in the United States and Canada with a Romanian parent, grandparent or great-grandparent ask the same question: can I become a Romanian citizen, and with it a citizen of the European Union? For many families the answer is yes. Romanian law allows people who lost their citizenship through no fault of their own, and their descendants, to regain it.

The application itself is a set of forms. The hard part is almost always the **paper trail of the family**: you have to prove that your ancestor was a Romanian citizen and that you descend from that person, generation by generation. This guide explains which documents usually make up that trail and where to look for them. It is not legal advice.

**Important:** the law, the list of documents and the procedure change from time to time. Before you file, check the current requirements with the National Citizenship Authority (Autoritatea Națională pentru Cetățenie, ANC) or with the Romanian consulate that covers your state or province.

## Who can apply

Article 11 of the Romanian citizenship law (Law 21/1991, as rewritten by Law 14/2025) covers two groups:

1. **Former Romanian citizens** who lost their citizenship for reasons not attributable to them, or whose citizenship was withdrawn against their will.
2. **Their descendants up to the third degree**: children, grandchildren and great-grandchildren.

Applicants must also be adults, loyal to the Romanian state and of good conduct. Descendants have to prove they know Romanian, usually with a language certificate at level B1 or higher, or with school records showing at least three years of study in Romanian. Former citizens themselves and applicants over 65 are exempt from the language proof.

In practice, this route fits families whose ancestor was a Romanian citizen and lost that citizenship: people who left Romania during communism and were stripped of citizenship, and the people of Bessarabia, northern Bukovina and the Hertsa region, who became Soviet citizens in 1940.

### The question to settle first: was your ancestor a Romanian citizen?

Many Romanian-speaking emigrants who reached America before the First World War came from Transylvania, Banat, Crișana or Bukovina, which were then part of Austria-Hungary. Romania as you know it on the map took shape only in 1918. An ancestor who left a village near Sibiu in 1905 was a Hungarian or Austrian subject, not a Romanian citizen. Check the dates and the borders before you start collecting documents; the guide to the [emigration waves](/ghid/romanian-immigration-waves-us-canada) has the timeline.

## What you need to prove

Every application rests on three links:

1. **Your ancestor was a Romanian citizen.** Usually through a birth or marriage record issued by Romanian authorities, or another Romanian document: an old identity card, passport, military booklet, property deed or diploma.
2. **The chain of descent.** A birth certificate for every generation between the ancestor and you, each naming the parents.
3. **Your own identity.** Your birth certificate, your marriage certificate if your name changed, and your passport.

## The ancestor's documents

The most common foundation is the ancestor's **birth or marriage record** from a period when the place belonged to Romania.

- **Romania today:** the town hall (primăria) of the place of birth holds civil records from roughly the last 100 years; older registers are in the National Archives. See [how to request a civil record](/ghid/cum-ceri-un-act-de-stare-civila) and [finding your grandfather's birth certificate](/ghid/certificatul-de-nastere-al-bunicului).
- **Bessarabia (Republic of Moldova):** civil registry archives and the National Archives of Moldova; for 1918–1940 the records were kept under Romanian administration. See [Bessarabia's parish registers](/ghid/basarabia-registrele-parohiale).
- **Northern Bukovina and Hertsa (Ukraine):** mainly the regional archive in Chernivtsi. See [Bukovina registers and archives](/ghid/bucovina-registre-si-arhive).

If you do not know the village, start there: the ancestor's US or Canadian naturalization papers, ship manifests and draft cards often name it. The guide on [finding your village of origin](/ghid/cum-afli-satul-de-origine) shows where to look.

## The documents of each generation in between

For every generation you need a birth record that shows the parents. When a name changed, you also need the document that explains the change: a marriage certificate, a court order or a naturalization certificate showing the new name.

American and Canadian documents usually have to be **apostilled** before Romanian authorities accept them. In the United States, the apostille comes from the Secretary of State of the state that issued the document (or the US Department of State for federal documents). In Canada, which joined the Apostille Convention in 2024, it comes from Global Affairs Canada or the competent provincial authority. Documents in English also need a certified Romanian translation.

## The problem of names written differently

This is the most common obstacle. Your great-grandfather is "Gheorghe Rusu" in a Romanian register, "George Russu" on his naturalization papers and "George Russ" in the census. Authorities compare the documents to decide whether they describe the same person.

What helps:

- documents that link the two spellings: a naturalization certificate that names the birthplace, a marriage record with both parents, a church or court document that states the former name;
- the same date and place of birth repeated across documents;
- a family tree with every document attached to the right person, so the chain is easy to follow.

Our guide on [Romanian surnames in America](/ghid/romanian-surnames-changed-in-america) explains the usual spelling changes and how to search under each one.

## How to keep the paper trail in order

A citizenship file often takes months, and the same documents are needed again later for a passport, a marriage registration or a sibling's application. Keep them organized from the start:

1. Build the tree from you back to the ancestor, one person per generation, with dates and places.
2. Attach a scan of every document to the person it belongs to, with a note on where the original is.
3. Write down every spelling of every name and the document where it appears.
4. Invite the relatives who are also applying, so a cousin does not request the same record twice.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I apply if my great-grandfather left Romania in 1905?

Only if he was a Romanian citizen when he left. Someone born in Transylvania or Bukovina before 1918 was a subject of Austria-Hungary, not of Romania, so this route usually does not apply. Someone born in the Old Kingdom (Wallachia, Moldavia, Dobruja) may be different; ask the consulate.

### Do I need to speak Romanian?

Descendants have to prove knowledge of Romanian, usually with a B1 certificate or with at least three years of school in Romanian. Former citizens and applicants over 65 are exempt.

### Where do I file from the US or Canada?

The law allows filing at the Romanian embassy or consulate in the country where you live. Each consulate publishes its own appointment system and checklist.

### My parents were born in Romania after 1990. Is this the right route?

Probably not. If a parent is still a Romanian citizen, you may be a citizen by birth and only need to register your birth with the Romanian authorities. Ask the consulate which procedure fits you.

### Do I need the original documents?

You usually file certified copies, apostilled and translated, and present originals when asked. Keep the originals safe and the scans in one place.
