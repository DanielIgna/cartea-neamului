Maybe your parents left Romania in the 1980s, maybe your grandmother came to London in 2010, or maybe all you have is a great-grandfather's name and the word "Transylvania". Whatever your starting point, finding your ancestors in Romania follows the same path: **from the people who remember, to the village, to the archive that holds the village's registers.**

This guide walks through that path step by step. You don't need to speak Romanian to begin, and you don't need to travel to Romania for most of it.

## Step 1: Write down what the family remembers

Before any archive, talk to the oldest relatives you have, in Romania and abroad. For each person, write down:

- full name, and for women the **maiden name**;
- approximate year of birth, marriage and death;
- the **village** or town, as precisely as possible;
- brothers and sisters, and who married whom;
- religion, because it tells you which church registers to search.

Ask your questions in Romanian if the relative prefers; many grandparents remember far more in their own language. Our list of [questions to ask Romanian grandparents](/ghid/questions-to-ask-romanian-grandparents) helps you start.

## Step 2: Gather the documents already in the house

Old passports, identity cards, work books, marriage certificates, military booklets, letters and photographs with writing on the back are often in a drawer somewhere. Each of them can give a date, a village or a parent's name. Photograph them before anything else.

## Step 3: Find the exact village

The village is the key that opens everything else. Romanian records are kept **by place**: the town hall of the commune for recent records, the county branch of the National Archives for older ones. A name alone is not enough.

If the family only remembers a region or a county, look at:

- marriage records and death certificates of emigrants, which often give the place of birth;
- immigration and naturalisation files in the country where the family settled;
- gravestones, which sometimes name the village.

Remember that villages in Transylvania and Banat had Hungarian and German names too, and many villages were renamed in the 20th century. See [how to find a Romanian village under its old names](/ghid/romanian-village-names-transylvania).

## Step 4: Find out which archive holds the registers

Once you know the village, find the commune and county it belongs to **today**. Then:

- **records from roughly the last 100 years** are at the civil status office of the town hall;
- **older civil and parish registers** are at the county branch of the National Archives (*Arhivele Naționale*).

Each village page on Cartea Neamului shows the commune, the county and the archive. Our guide to [Romanian birth records](/ghid/romanian-birth-records) explains what each office can issue.

## Step 5: Search the registers

There are two kinds of registers, depending on the year:

- **Civil registers**, kept by the town hall: from December 1865 in Wallachia and Moldavia, from 1 October 1895 in Transylvania, Banat, Crișana and Maramureș.
- **Parish registers**, kept by each church, for the years before that. A family's religion decides which registers you need: Orthodox, Greek Catholic, Roman Catholic, Reformed, Lutheran or Jewish. See [Romanian church records](/ghid/romanian-church-records).

Many of these registers were microfilmed and can be viewed for free on **FamilySearch**. Look for your village there first. If it is not online, write to the county archive with the name, the village and the approximate years.

## Step 6: Use censuses and land records to fill gaps

When a register is missing, other sources help: the Hungarian censuses of 1869–1910 for Transylvania, the conscriptions and *catagrafii* of the 18th and 19th centuries, the general census of 1930, and land and property records. See our guide to [old censuses](/ghid/recensamintele-vechi).

## Step 7: Build the tree with the family in Romania

Relatives still in Romania are your best research partners: they know the village, the neighbours, the graves, and they can go to the town hall for you with a power of attorney. On Cartea Neamului the tree is **shared and private**: relatives in Romania fill it in in Romanian, you read it in English, and every name, photograph and story stays in one place.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to speak Romanian to find my Romanian ancestors?

No. You can build the tree and search FamilySearch in English. For letters to archives, a short, clear email in English usually gets an answer, and a relative or translator can help with Romanian.

### What is the single most important thing to find?

The exact village. Romanian records are organised by place, so once you know the village, you know where to search.

### Can I request Romanian records without going to Romania?

Yes: through a Romanian consulate (appointments on econsulat.ro), through a relative with a power of attorney, or by writing to the National Archives for registers older than 100 years.

### How far back can I go?

Most families can reach the early or mid-19th century through civil and parish registers. Earlier than that depends on the region and on whether the registers survived.

### Is my family tree public?

No. A tree on Cartea Neamului is private by default; only the relatives you invite can see it.
