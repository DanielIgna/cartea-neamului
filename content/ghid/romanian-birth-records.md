A birth record is usually the first Romanian document a family abroad goes looking for. Sometimes it is for a citizenship application, sometimes for a passport, and very often simply because someone wants to know where a grandparent really came from. The good news is that Romania kept birth records for a long time, and most of them still exist. The harder part is knowing **which office holds the record you need**, because that depends on the year and on the region.

This guide explains where Romanian birth records are, what they contain, and how to ask for a copy when you live outside Romania.

## Two kinds of birth records

Before 1865 in the south and east of Romania, and before 1895 in Transylvania and Banat, births were written down by the priest in the **parish register**: the record of a baptism, which normally also gives the date of birth. After that, the state took over and births were registered at the town hall, in the **civil registers** (*registre de stare civilă*).

- **Wallachia and Moldavia (the Old Kingdom)**: from the 1830s, priests kept registers in a form required by the state; from December 1865, births were registered at the town hall.
- **Transylvania, Banat, Crișana and Maramureș**: state civil registration began on **1 October 1895**, under Hungarian law, and the registers were kept in Hungarian until 1918–1919. Before 1895, look in the parish registers of each church.
- **Bukovina, Bessarabia and Dobruja** had Austrian, Russian and Ottoman administrations before 1918 (Dobruja before 1878), so church registers served as birth records for a long time. What survives differs from place to place.

## Where the record is today

The rule of thumb is the **100-year line**:

- **Births from the last 100 years or so** are at the civil status office (*serviciul de stare civilă*) of the town hall where the birth was registered. This is where certificates for living people and recent ancestors are issued.
- **Older registers** are transferred to the county branch of the **Romanian National Archives** (*Arhivele Naționale*). The archives can issue a certified copy or an extract of the entry.
- **Online images**: many parish and civil registers were microfilmed and can be viewed for free on FamilySearch, with a free account. Use them to find the entry first; then ask the archive for an official copy if you need one.

> Villages changed names and counties several times in the 20th century. Before you write to anyone, check which commune and county the village belongs to today. Each village page on Cartea Neamului shows the commune, the county and the county archive.

## What a Romanian birth record says

The form changed over time, but an older birth entry usually gives:

- the date of birth, and often the date it was registered;
- the child's first name and sex;
- the parents' names, often with their age, occupation and religion;
- the house number or street, which helps a lot in large villages;
- witnesses or the person who declared the birth, often a relative or neighbour;
- notes added later in the margin: a marriage, a death, a change of name.

Those margin notes are often the most valuable part. They can tell you where and when someone married, even if they moved to another county.

## Languages and dates you will meet

- In the Old Kingdom, registers from the 1830s to the 1860s are often written in **Romanian Cyrillic** or in the transitional alphabet. See our guide to [the Romanian Cyrillic alphabet](/ghid/scrierea-chirilica-romaneasca).
- In Transylvania and Banat, records from 1895 to 1918 are in **Hungarian**, with first names translated: Ioan appears as János, Gheorghe as György, Maria as Mária.
- Until 1919, dates in the Old Kingdom follow the **Julian calendar**, thirteen days behind the modern one in the 20th century.

## How to request a copy from abroad

There are three routes, and most families use one of the first two.

### 1. Through a Romanian consulate

Romania's embassies and consulates can forward requests for civil status certificates and issue documents for Romanian citizens. Appointments are booked on **econsulat.ro**. This is the usual route if you are a Romanian citizen asking for your own birth certificate or that of a parent.

### 2. Through a relative in Romania

A relative in Romania can go to the town hall for you with a **power of attorney** (*procură*), which you sign at the consulate or in front of a notary and, outside the EU, with an apostille. This is often the fastest way, especially in small villages where the civil status clerk knows the registers well.

### 3. Directly to the National Archives

For registers **older than 100 years**, write to the county branch of the National Archives. Give the full name, the approximate year, the village and, if you know them, the parents' names. The archive will search the register and tell you what it costs to issue a copy. Requests can usually be sent by email.

## Using a Romanian birth certificate abroad

- **Inside the European Union**, civil status documents circulate without an apostille, and you can ask for a **multilingual standard form** that translates the headings, so you often don't need a certified translation.
- **Outside the EU** (the UK, the US, Canada, Australia, New Zealand), a Romanian certificate normally needs an **apostille** and a certified translation.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I get my great-grandfather's birth record if he was born in 1900?

Yes. A birth from 1900 is more than 100 years old, so the register is normally at the county branch of the National Archives. Write to them with his name, the village and the approximate year.

### I don't know the village. Where do I start?

Start with documents the family already has: a marriage certificate, an immigration or naturalisation file, a gravestone, letters. Our guide on [how to find your ancestral village](/ghid/cum-afli-satul-de-origine) shows where the village usually hides.

### Is the birth record in English?

No. Romanian records are in Romanian, and depending on the year and region they may be in Hungarian, German, Russian or Romanian Cyrillic. The archives issue copies in Romanian; you will need a translation for official use outside Romania.

### Do I need a birth record for Romanian citizenship by descent?

Yes. You need records showing the link between you and the Romanian parent or grandparent, generation by generation. See [documents for Romanian citizenship by descent](/ghid/romanian-citizenship-by-descent-documents).

### Can my relatives in Romania help me build the tree?

Yes. On Cartea Neamului the tree is shared: relatives in Romania add what they know in Romanian, you read it in English, and every name and village stays in one place.
