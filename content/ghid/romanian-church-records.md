For most Romanian families, everything before the 1860s or 1890s is in **church registers**: baptisms, marriages and burials written down by the parish priest or pastor. They are the oldest records most families can reach, and in many villages they go back to the early 19th century.

The question is always the same: **which church?** Romania has been home to many confessions, and each kept its own registers, in its own language. This guide explains which registers to look for and where they are today.

## Why church records matter

State civil registration began in December 1865 in Wallachia and Moldavia and on 1 October 1895 in Transylvania, Banat, Crișana and Maramureș. Before those dates, the parish register is usually the only record of a birth, a marriage or a death. Even after them, church registers often add details the town hall did not write down, such as godparents.

## Which church kept your family's records

### Orthodox

Most Romanians were Orthodox, especially in Wallachia, Moldavia, Dobruja and much of Transylvania. Older Orthodox registers are often written in **Romanian Cyrillic**; the Latin alphabet replaced it gradually in the 1860s. See our guide to [the Romanian Cyrillic alphabet](/ghid/scrierea-chirilica-romaneasca).

### Greek Catholic

In Transylvania, Banat, Crișana and Maramureș, many Romanian villages were Greek Catholic. Their registers are often in **Latin** or in Romanian in the Latin alphabet earlier than Orthodox registers in the same area, and some are in Hungarian. In 1948 the Greek Catholic Church was banned; its registers ended up partly in the state archives and partly in the Orthodox parishes that took over the churches, and some were lost. The Church was recognised again in 1990.

### Roman Catholic

Roman Catholic registers cover Hungarians, the Banat Swabians and other Germans, the Csángós of Moldavia and others. They are usually in **Latin**, later in Hungarian or German.

### Reformed and Unitarian

Many Hungarian families in Transylvania were Reformed (Calvinist) or Unitarian. Their registers are usually in **Hungarian**.

### Lutheran

The Transylvanian Saxons were Lutheran, and their registers are in **German**. Many Saxon parish archives are well preserved.

### Jewish

Jewish communities kept their own birth, marriage and death registers, and in some periods the state kept separate registers for Jews. See our guide to [Jewish ancestors from Romania](/ghid/stramosi-evrei-din-romania).

> Mixed villages were common. A Romanian Greek Catholic and a Hungarian Reformed family could live on the same street, and their records are in different books. If you can't find someone, check the other churches in the village.

## What a church register entry says

- **Baptism**: date of birth and baptism, the child's name, the parents, often the house number, and the **godparents** (*nași*), who were frequently relatives.
- **Marriage**: the bride and groom, their ages and villages, often their parents, and the witnesses.
- **Burial**: the date, age at death, sometimes the cause and the surviving spouse.

Ages at death were declared by relatives and are often rounded, so treat them as approximate.

## Where the registers are today

- Many parish registers were taken over by the state and are now at the **county branches of the National Archives**, usually alongside the civil registers.
- Some registers are still with the parishes or the church's diocesan archives.
- A large number were microfilmed and can be viewed for free on **FamilySearch**, organised by place.

Start with FamilySearch, searching by the village's name under the county. If the village isn't there, write to the county branch of the National Archives and ask which registers they hold for that village and confession.

## Dates and calendars

Until 1919, Romania used the **Julian calendar**, which in the 19th century ran twelve days, and in the 20th century thirteen days, behind the Gregorian one. Catholic and Protestant registers in Transylvania used the Gregorian calendar earlier. If two records of the same event disagree by about twelve or thirteen days, this is usually why.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I know which church my ancestors belonged to?

Ask the family, look at old documents (many civil records also note religion), and look at the village: the confession of most villagers is a good first guess. The 1930 census results show the confessions present in each village.

### Are Romanian church records online?

Many are, as images on FamilySearch. They are not always indexed by name, so you will browse the register page by page for your village and years.

### What language are the records in?

It depends on the church and the period: Romanian (in Cyrillic or Latin letters), Latin, Hungarian, German or Hebrew and Yiddish.

### My ancestors were Greek Catholic. Where are their records after 1948?

Partly at the National Archives, partly in Orthodox parishes, and partly lost. Ask the county archive first.

### Can I build the tree with relatives who read these languages?

Yes. On Cartea Neamului each relative reads the tree in their own language, and you can attach photos of register pages to each person.
