Romanians did not come to North America all at once. They came in waves, each with its own reasons, its own regions of origin and its own places of settlement. Knowing which wave your family belonged to tells you where to look for records, what language they may be written in, and which questions to ask the older relatives.

This guide follows the waves in order, from the peasants of the 1890s to the families who left after 1989.

## Before the great migration

A few Romanians reached America long before the 1890s. In 1748 Samuel Damian, a priest from Transylvania, gave electrical demonstrations in South Carolina, and Benjamin Franklin mentions him in letters. Romanian-born officers served in the Union Army during the Civil War. These were individuals, though, not communities. The great migration started later.

## Wave 1: peasants and workers, 1895–1920

Between 1895 and 1920, about **145,000 Romanians** entered the United States. Most were young men from **Transylvania and Banat**, then part of Austria-Hungary, with smaller groups from **Bukovina** and the Old Kingdom. They left behind small plots of land, economic hardship and, in Transylvania, pressure to give up their language under Hungarian rule.

Most planned to work for a few years, save money and go home to buy land. Many did exactly that: tens of thousands returned, and the Romanian American population was about 85,000 by 1920.

**Where they settled in the US.** They went where the factories were: the steel mills of **Ohio** and **Pennsylvania**, the car plants of **Detroit**, the mills and packing houses of **Chicago** and of **Gary and Indiana Harbor** in Indiana, and the coal mines. In **Cleveland**, about 12,000 Romanians lived on the west side by the start of the First World War, many between West 45th and West 65th streets. They built the first Romanian Orthodox church in the United States, St. Mary's, organized in 1904, and the Romanian newspaper *America* has appeared there since 1906.

**Where they settled in Canada.** Canada was different: there, Romanians came for land. From 1896, the government promoted homesteads in the West, and families from Bukovina arrived on the prairies. In 1898 the first two families from the village of **Boian** in Bukovina settled in Alberta; about a hundred more followed and the place is still called **Boian, Alberta**. In Saskatchewan, Romanian farmers settled around **Regina, Limerick, Dysart, Kayville, Flintoft and Canora**, and in Manitoba around **Inglis**. The parish of St. Nicholas in Regina, founded in 1902, is considered the oldest Romanian Orthodox parish in North America.

**Romanian Jews.** In the same years, about 75,000 Jews left Romania between 1881 and 1914, most of them for **New York City**, where they founded their own congregations and aid societies on the Lower East Side.

**What records to look for:** arrival lists at New York (Ellis Island) and the Canadian ports, homestead files in the Canadian West, naturalization papers, the 1910–1940 US censuses, and records of mutual aid societies. In Romania, look in the parish registers of the home village, often written in Hungarian or German before 1918.

## Wave 2: a slower stream between the wars, 1920–1940

After 1918 Transylvania, Bukovina and Banat became part of Romania, and there were fewer reasons to leave. In the United States, the Immigration Act of 1924 limited Romanian arrivals to a quota of 603 people a year. Then came the Great Depression. Romanian immigration slowed to a trickle, and many of those who came were relatives joining families already settled.

In Canada, emigration continued on a smaller scale, still largely to the prairies, and later to Ontario and Quebec.

## Wave 3: refugees and exiles after the Second World War, 1945–1960

When the communist regime took power in Romania after 1945, a new kind of emigrant appeared: the **political refugee**. Officers, lawyers, doctors, engineers, writers and former officials who had escaped or were abroad when the borders closed could not go home. About **30,000** came to the United States as refugees and exiles under the Displaced Persons Act of 1948 and later laws. In Cleveland alone, about 2,000 arrived from 1948.

In Canada, many of these professionals settled in **Montreal, Toronto, Hamilton, London and Windsor**, which is why Romanian communities in Ontario and Quebec grew during these years.

**What records to look for:** displaced persons files, refugee camp records in Austria, Germany and Italy, and naturalization papers. In Romania, look in the archives of the former secret police: our guide to [the Securitate file](/ghid/dosarul-de-la-securitate-al-bunicului) explains how to ask.

## Wave 4: leaving a closed country, 1960–1989

Under communism, leaving Romania was difficult. Passports were kept by the police, and people who applied to emigrate often lost their jobs and were harassed. Still, emigration continued through a few narrow channels:

- **Family reunification**, usually after years of waiting.
- **Ethnic Germans** (Banat Swabians and Transylvanian Saxons), who left mostly for West Germany; some later moved on to the United States and Canada.
- **Romanian Jews**, who left mostly for Israel and the United States.
- **Escapes across the border**, often across the Danube into Yugoslavia or into Hungary, followed by months in refugee camps before reaching America or Canada.

From 1975, US trade relations with Romania were tied to its emigration policy under the Jackson–Vanik amendment, which opened the door to more family reunifications. Romania gave up that status in 1988.

**What records to look for:** refugee and resettlement files, family letters and parcels, and the stories of the people themselves. Many of them are still alive: record their memories while you can.

## Wave 5: after 1989

After the revolution of December 1989, Romanians could travel freely. In the 1990s many went to **New York, Los Angeles, Chicago**, and later to **Atlanta, Phoenix, Florida and the Pacific Northwest**. Canada attracted skilled immigrants, especially French-speaking ones to **Montreal**: in the early 2000s, more than 5,500 people from Romania became permanent residents of Canada every year, with a peak of 5,658 in 2004.

Today, 425,738 Americans reported Romanian ancestry in the 2023 American Community Survey, and 215,885 Canadians reported Romanian origin in the 2021 census.

## How to find your family's wave

Ask the older relatives three questions: who left first, in what year, and from which village. Then match the answer to the wave:

- **before 1920:** look for arrival lists, homestead files and parish registers in Hungarian or German;
- **1945–1960:** look for refugee and displaced persons files;
- **1960–1989:** look for family letters, reunification papers and escape stories;
- **after 1989:** the people are usually alive: record their story now.

## Frequently asked questions

### When did most Romanians come to the United States?

The largest early wave came between 1895 and 1920, mostly from Transylvania and Banat. Another large wave came after 1989.

### Why did so many early Romanian immigrants go back home?

Many came only to earn money and buy land at home. Tens of thousands returned in the years before and after the First World War.

### Where did the first Romanians in Canada settle?

On the prairies: in Saskatchewan around Regina, Limerick, Dysart and Kayville, in Manitoba around Inglis, and in Alberta at Boian, founded in 1898 by families from Bukovina.

### Could people leave Romania during communism?

Only with great difficulty. Some left legally through family reunification or as ethnic Germans and Jews; others escaped across the border and became refugees.

### Where can I find my ancestor's arrival record?

In the passenger lists of the port of arrival: New York for most of the US, and Halifax, Quebec City or Montreal for Canada.
