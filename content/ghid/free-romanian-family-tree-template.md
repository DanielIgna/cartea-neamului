A family tree template is the simplest way to begin: one sheet of paper, your name at the bottom, your parents above you, then grandparents and great-grandparents. For families with Romanian roots in the United States or Canada, it is also the moment when the questions start. What was grandma's maiden name? Which village did great-grandfather come from? Was the family name always spelled this way?

This guide gives you a free template and explains how to fill it in for a Romanian family, so the sheet becomes the start of real research instead of a decoration on the fridge.

## The free template

Our [printable family tree](/unelte/arbore-printabil) has room for three, four or five generations. Choose the size, print it and fill it in by hand. It is free and needs no account.

- **Three generations** (you, parents, grandparents): good for a child's school project or a first conversation with grandparents.
- **Four generations** (up to great-grandparents): most families can fill this in with the help of the oldest relatives.
- **Five generations** (up to great-great-grandparents): this usually needs documents from Romania, because few people remember names that far back.

Our [generations calculator](/unelte/calculator-generatii) shows how many ancestors each generation holds and roughly when they were born.

## How to fill it in for a Romanian family

### Write names as they appear in Romanian records

Romanian documents use Romanian first names: Ioan, not John; Gheorghe, not George; Maria, Ana, Elena. Write the Romanian form in the box and the American form next to it in small letters. When you search records in Romania later, you will need the Romanian one.

### Always write the maiden name

In Romania, women traditionally took their husband's surname at marriage. On the template, write a woman under her **maiden name**, the one she was born with. That is the name you will find in her birth record and the one that leads to her parents. See [finding your grandmother's maiden name](/ghid/cum-gasesti-numele-de-fata-al-bunicii).

### Write the village, not only the country

"Romania" is not enough to find a record. Write the village or town and the county (județ) for every person born there. Romanian civil and parish records are kept locally, so the village is the key. If you do not know it, the guide on [finding your village of origin](/ghid/cum-afli-satul-de-origine) shows where it hides in American and Canadian papers: naturalization records, ship manifests, draft cards, obituaries.

### Note every spelling of the surname

A family called Moldovan in Romania may appear as Moldavan, Moldowan or Moldvan in America. Write each version on the back of the sheet. The guide on [Romanian surnames in America](/ghid/romanian-surnames-changed-in-america) explains how names changed and how to search under each spelling.

### Write dates even when you are not sure

"About 1920" is more useful than an empty box. Mark uncertain dates with "abt." and correct them when you find a document.

## Who to ask first

Start with the oldest relatives, and do it soon. A short phone call with a great-aunt can fill a whole branch. Our list of [40 questions to ask your Romanian grandparents](/ghid/questions-to-ask-romanian-grandparents) gives you the questions in English and in Romanian.

Relatives in Romania often know what the American side has forgotten: the nickname of the family in the village, the cousins who stayed, the graves in the village cemetery.

## When the paper is not enough

A printed template is perfect for the first evening. After that, most families run into the same limits:

- the sheet fills up, and there is no room for brothers, sisters, cousins and second marriages;
- only one person holds the paper, and relatives in other cities or in Romania cannot add what they know;
- there is no place for photos, documents or the voices of grandparents.

That is when an online tree helps. On Cartea Neamului you start the tree in the browser, invite relatives with a link sent on WhatsApp or email, and everyone adds what they know in their own language. Photos, documents and short voice recordings stay on each person's profile, and the tree stays private, visible only to the people you invite. See [how to build a free family tree online, step by step](/ghid/arbore-genealogic-gratuit-online-pas-cu-pas).

You can also do both: fill in the template at the kitchen table with your grandparents, then copy it into the online tree, so the whole family can continue it.

## Ideas for using the template

- **A school project.** Many teachers ask for a three-generation tree. A Romanian-American child can add the village names and the Romanian first names; it becomes a lesson in where the family comes from.
- **A family reunion.** Print one large sheet, put it on the wall and let every relative write what they know. Photograph it at the end. See [organizing a family reunion](/ghid/reuniune-de-familie).
- **A gift for grandparents.** A tree filled in together, with their names in Romanian, means more than any object.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the template really free?

Yes. The printable tree and the generations calculator are free and need no account.

### How many generations can I realistically fill in?

Most families fill in three or four generations from memory. The fifth usually needs records from Romania: civil registers from the last century and parish registers for older generations. See [how many generations you can find](/ghid/cate-generatii-poti-afla).

### Should I write names in Romanian or in English?

Both. The Romanian form helps you search records in Romania; the English form helps you search American and Canadian records.

### What if I do not know my great-grandparents' names?

Leave the box empty and write what you know around it: a village, a year, a nickname. Ask the oldest relatives first, then look at naturalization papers and obituaries, which often name parents.

### Can relatives in Romania fill in the tree with me?

On paper, only if they are in the same room. In an online tree you can invite them, and each person writes in their own language.
