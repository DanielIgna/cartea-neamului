If your surname ends in **-escu**, **-eanu** or **-aru**, it almost certainly comes from Romania. Many people with Romanian roots in Britain, Australia, the US or Germany know that much. What fewer know is that the ending of a Romanian surname often says something specific: whose son the first bearer was, which region he came from, or what he did for a living.

This guide explains the most common endings and names, and how to use them as a clue, not as proof, when you look for your ancestors.

## Surnames came late

Fixed family names, passed from father to children, are fairly recent in Romania. Until the 19th century, many villagers were known by their first name, by their father's name ("Ion al lui Vasile", Ion son of Vasile) or by a nickname. Names became fixed gradually, through registers, the army and school, and in Wallachia and Moldavia a law of 1895 required everyone to have a family name.

That is why two brothers born in the 1850s can appear with different surnames, and why the name your family carries today may be only four or five generations old.

## The endings and what they mean

### -escu: "son of"

**-escu** is the classic Romanian [patronymic](/glosar/patronimic) ending: it means "belonging to the family of". **Ionescu** is the son of Ion, **Petrescu** the son of Petre, **Constantinescu** the son of Constantin. **Popescu**, the most famous Romanian surname, comes from *popă*, an old word for the village priest: the priest's son.

These names are most frequent in Wallachia and Oltenia (southern Romania), but they spread everywhere in the 20th century.

### -eanu and -anu: "from that place"

**-eanu** (or **-anu**) usually shows where a family came from. **Munteanu** is someone from the mountains or from Muntenia, **Moldoveanu** from Moldavia, **Ardeleanu** from Transylvania (*Ardeal*), **Ungureanu** someone who came from the Hungarian-ruled lands across the Carpathians. A name like **Bucureșteanu** points to Bucharest, **Olteanu** to Oltenia.

### -aru and -ar: the trade

Many surnames are old occupations: **Morar** or **Moraru** (miller), **Rotaru** (wheelwright), **Olaru** (potter), **Ciobanu** (shepherd), **Lăutaru** (village musician), **Fieraru** (blacksmith), **Cojocaru** (maker of sheepskin coats).

### -iuc and -ciuc: Bukovina and the north

Endings like **-iuc** and **-ciuc** are frequent in Bukovina and northern Moldavia, where Romanian and Ukrainian families lived side by side for centuries.

### -ache and -ică

**-ache** (Iordache, Costache, Manolache) is common in Moldavia and Wallachia and reflects the Greek influence of the 18th and 19th centuries. **-ică** is a diminutive (Mitică, Costică) and appears in surnames formed from pet names.

### Short names from Transylvania

In Transylvania and Maramureș many surnames are short and plain: **Pop**, **Moldovan**, **Rus**, **Lazăr**, **Man**, **Mureșan** (from the Mureș river). **Pop** is the Transylvanian form of the priest's name; south and east of the Carpathians the same idea gives **Popa** and **Popescu**.

## Names from nicknames and colours

A large group of surnames started as nicknames: **Lungu** (tall), **Scurtu** (short), **Negru** (black, for dark hair), **Albu** (white, fair), **Roșu** or **Rusu** (red-haired, though Rusu can also mean "Russian"), **Sârbu** (Serb), **Grecu** (Greek), **Turcu** (Turk). They tell you how neighbours once saw the first bearer, not necessarily where the family came from.

## How a surname changed abroad and in old records

- In Transylvania between 1895 and 1918, civil records were in Hungarian, so a **Pop** may be written **Pap** and **Moldovan** as **Moldován**.
- Emigrants often dropped the diacritics: **Ștefănescu** became **Stefanescu**, **Mureșan** became **Muresan**, **Țurcanu** became **Turcanu** or even **Tzurcanu**.
- In English-speaking countries, endings were sometimes shortened or anglicised. See [how Romanian surnames changed in America](/ghid/romanian-surnames-changed-in-america).

## Use the surname as a clue, not a map

A surname can point you to a region, but it will not find your village on its own. Thousands of unrelated families are called Popescu or Pop. What works is the combination: **surname + first names + approximate year + any place name** the family remembers. ## Frequently asked questions

### What does Popescu mean?

It comes from *popă*, the village priest, plus the ending -escu: "the priest's son" or "from the priest's family". It is one of the most common surnames in Romania.

### Does a name ending in -escu mean my family is from Bucharest?

Not necessarily. -escu names are most frequent in Wallachia and Oltenia, but by the 20th century they were found all over Romania.

### My surname has no Romanian ending. Can it still be Romanian?

Yes. Many Transylvanian surnames are short (Pop, Rus, Man), and many come from trades or nicknames without a special ending. Hungarian, German, Ukrainian and other names are also part of Romania's history.

### Why do my ancestors have different surnames in different records?

Before names became fixed, people could be recorded by their father's name, a nickname or the name of the house. Clerks also spelled names in Hungarian, German or Russian depending on the period.

### How do I find where my surname comes from?

Start with the living family, then the oldest documents you have. Our [surname pages](/nume) and [guide to Romanian surnames](/ghid/de-unde-vine-numele-tau-de-familie) help you narrow down the region.
