If you are living in the United States or Canada with Romanian roots, you probably know a familiar feeling: life moves fast, holidays come and go over video calls, and the stories of where your family actually came from slowly shrink into a few short anecdotes.

Whether your parents immigrated a few decades ago or your great-grandparents arrived generations back from Transylvania, Moldavia, Wallachia or Banat, rebuilding your Romanian family tree from across the ocean is easier than you think, as long as you start in the right place.

Here is a practical, step-by-step guide to tracing your Romanian ancestry from North America and turning scattered memories into a lasting family heirloom.

## 1. Start with living memory before searching archives

Many people assume genealogy begins in dusty archives or online census databases. In reality, your best archive is still sitting at the kitchen table, either in your own home or in a village back in Romania.

Before looking up historical records, gather the oral history of your living relatives:

- **Ask about villages, not just counties.** In Romania, family identity is deeply tied to the exact village (*sat*) or commune (*comună*). Knowing whether your great-grandparents came from a specific village in Maramureș, Bucovina or Bihor unlocks almost everything else, because Romanian records are kept by place. Our guide on [finding your family's village of origin](/ghid/cum-afli-satul-de-origine) (in Romanian) walks through the clues.
- **Write down maiden names and nicknames.** In smaller Romanian communities, families were often known by a house nickname (*poreclă*) alongside their official surname. Ask older relatives about these: they are vital clues when you talk to distant cousins who still live in the village.
- **Collect old documents and photos.** Birth certificates (*certificat de naștere*), marriage records, military booklets (*livret militar*) and old black-and-white postcards often have dates, parishes and signatures written on the back.
- **Note the religion of each branch.** Orthodox, Greek-Catholic, Roman-Catholic, Reformed, Lutheran or Jewish: before civil registration, births and marriages were written down by the local parish or community, so the religion tells you which register to look for.

**Tip:** don't wait until you have all the answers. Start with yourself, your parents and your grandparents, and leave the unknown branches blank for other relatives to fill in later.

## 2. Bridge the distance between North America and Romania

One of the biggest challenges for Romanian families split between the US, Canada and Europe is that no single person holds the whole story. Your aunt in Bucharest might have the wedding photos from 1954, your cousin in Chicago might know the dates of immigration, and your grandmother back home remembers the names of her own grandparents.

## 3. Record your grandparents' voices before it is too late

Dates and names tell you when someone lived, but a voice tells you who they were.

For children born and raised in the US or Canada, hearing their grandmother describe childhood winters in a Romanian village, a traditional recipe or the courage it took to build a new life creates a bond that no chart can match.

Try asking your parents or grandparents a few simple questions over the phone or during your next visit:

- What is your earliest memory from childhood?
- What was the house you grew up in like?
- How did you and Grandma (or Grandpa) meet?
- What traditions did your family keep for Easter and Christmas?
- Who in the family left first, and why?

## 4. How to research historical Romanian records from abroad

Once you have mapped three or four generations from family memory, you can go deeper into historical records.

### Romanian National Archives

The Romanian National Archives (*Arhivele Naționale ale României*) keep parish registers and civil registration books in their county branches (*Serviciile Județene*). As a rule, civil registers older than 100 years are handed over by the town halls to the archives, while newer ones stay at the local town hall. Knowing the exact village and the religion will point you to the right register. Our Romanian guide to [parish registers online](/ghid/registre-parohiale-online) lists what is already digitized.

### Transylvania and Banat records

If your ancestors lived in regions that were part of the Austro-Hungarian Empire before 1918, records from the late 19th and early 20th centuries may be written in Hungarian, German or Latin. Civil registration began there in October 1895, and many parish registers from earlier decades have been microfilmed and can be browsed online. See our guide to [Transylvanian registers from 1850 to 1914](/ghid/hpdt-registrele-din-transilvania) and the overview of [free public sources](/surse-publice).

### North American immigration records and ship manifests

Search the Castle Garden (before 1892) and Ellis Island (from 1892) arrival lists for New York, and the Canadian passenger lists for Halifax, Quebec City and Montreal. These lists often give the last place of residence, which is exactly the village you need. Keep in mind that names were written down by clerks in many forms, so search for Hungarian, German and Americanized spellings too. Our Romanian guide on [emigration to America](/ghid/emigrarea-in-america) explains what each column means.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to speak Romanian to trace my Romanian ancestors?

No, but it helps to learn a few key words: *naștere* (birth), *căsătorie* or *cununie* (marriage), *deces* (death), *sat* (village) and *parohie* (parish). Older records in Transylvania and Banat may also be in Hungarian, German or Latin.

### What is the most important piece of information to find first?

The exact village. Romanian records are organized by place, so a village name lets you find the right parish register and archive branch much faster than a county name alone.

### Can I request Romanian records from the US or Canada?

Yes. County branches of the National Archives answer written requests, and newer civil records can be requested from the town hall of the village, often with the help of a relative in Romania or through a Romanian consulate.

### Can my relatives in Romania and in North America work on the same tree?

Yes. You invite them with a link, each person chooses the site language, and everyone sees the same tree. What each relative writes stays in the language they wrote it in.

### Is my family tree public?

No. A tree on Cartea Neamului is private by default and only the relatives you invite can see it.
